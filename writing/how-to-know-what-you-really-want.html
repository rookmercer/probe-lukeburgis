How to Know What You Really Want

By Luke Burgis  ·  lukeburgis.com

Claire, a smart, ambitious student at Tulane University in Louisiana, was on track to have her pick of law schools, but she decided she'd like to get some real-world experience — and have some fun — in New Orleans first. She landed a job as a paralegal, spending her days researching expert witnesses to defend Big Pharma cases, and that's when the crisis came.

Claire had always loved cooking and learning about humanity through cuisine. She was like a female Anthony Bourdain trapped inside an overworked paralegal, and it was slowly making her life miserable. She began to entertain thoughts of leaving the law firm and working in a kitchen or a coffee shop until she could figure out how to make a career out of her lifelong interest in food.

But doubts haunted her. What would other people think? Maybe she's not that driven. Maybe she's not that smart, after all. Maybe she's lazy. What other people expected her to want to do — and her ability to meet those expectations — began to determine her self-worth.

Many people face dilemmas like Claire's. Each of us is occasionally overwhelmed by a multitude of competing desires: pursue job offer A or B? Start a new relationship or stay single? Sign up to run a marathon, or enjoy not getting up early to train? But life is full of marathons, and they don't necessarily involve running. It's good to know which desires to pursue and which ones to leave behind — to know which marathons are worth running.

The Source of Confused Desire

Before you can know what you really want, you have to understand where your wants come from. According to the French social theorist René Girard, whose work forms the basis for the field of mimetic theory, most of what we call desire is actually mimetic — imitated from models around us, rather than arising from some authentic inner source.

"The first question to ask of any strong desire: is this mine — or did I catch it from someone nearby?"

This doesn't mean our desires are fake or that we should ignore them. It means that the process of knowing what we really want requires a kind of archaeology — digging through the layers of borrowed desire to find the bedrock of something genuinely our own.

Girard distinguished between two zones of desire. In the first — what I call Celebristan — our models are distant: celebrities, historical figures, people in a different social world. Desire inspired by these models tends to be relatively stable and non-conflictual. You can admire a historical explorer without competing with one.

In the second zone — Freshmanistan — our models are close to us: peers, colleagues, neighbors, social media connections. Desire here is volatile because competition is possible. The closer the model, the more intense the mimetic pull — and the more likely we are to mistake borrowed desire for authentic wanting.

Four Diagnostic Questions

When you find yourself caught between competing desires, these four questions can help you locate what is genuinely yours:

1. Who wants this? Think carefully about who in your social world seems to want or value the thing you're pursuing. If the answer is "everyone I know" or "the people I most want to impress," treat this as a yellow flag. The desire may be genuine, but it may also be heavily shaped by social pressure.

2. Would I want this if no one knew? Imagine pursuing this goal — the career, the relationship, the purchase, the achievement — in complete anonymity. No one would know you had it. Would you still want it? If the answer is a flat no, the desire is probably more about status than substance.

3. How old is this desire? Mimetically acquired desires tend to be recent — they spike with social exposure and fade when the model disappears. Desires that have persisted across different social contexts, different life stages, different circles of friends, tend to be more authentically yours.

4. What would I give up for this? Genuine desire has a cost structure. Ask what you would willingly sacrifice to pursue it. The answer reveals something real about the desire's depth and origin.

Thick Desires and Thin Desires

The philosopher Charles Taylor distinguished between what he called "strong evaluations" and mere preferences. I find it useful to think about this distinction as the difference between thick desires and thin desires.

A thin desire is a preference with no roots — it floats on the surface of your life, easily replaced by another thin desire. Most consumer preferences are thin desires. Most social-media-driven wants are thin desires.

A thick desire is connected to your deepest sense of who you are and what matters. It has roots in your history, your relationships, your commitments. Claire's love of cooking and her desire to understand humanity through food — that was a thick desire. Her aspiration to be a lawyer, as she had come to understand it, was thin: it was borrowed, not built.

The goal is not to eliminate desire but to let the thick desires guide the thin ones — to let what genuinely matters to you organize the many smaller choices that make up a life.

The Practice of Wanting Well

Knowing what you really want is not a one-time achievement. It is a practice — an ongoing discipline of attention and honesty. Here is how to begin:

First, create distance from your models. Social media is a mimetic engine; spending time away from it helps clarify which desires are yours and which are ambient noise. Second, develop a practice of reflection — journaling, contemplative prayer, long walks without podcasts — that gives your inner life enough silence to become audible. Third, seek out mentors who embody thick desires rather than thin ones: people who know what they want and why, whose wanting is integrated into a genuine life rather than a performed one.

Claire eventually left the law firm. She went to culinary school, built a career in food journalism, and — last I heard — was happier than she had ever been. Not because she found the "right" answer, but because she learned to ask the right questions.
