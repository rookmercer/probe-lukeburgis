The Wonder of Collaboration: Finding a New Spark of Creativity with Liana Finck

By Luke Burgis  ·  lukeburgis.com

I reached out to Liana Finck during the early, dark days of the pandemic in April 2020. My fiancée, Claire, insisted that I do.

I had realized that my nonfiction book, Wanting — a book about a social phenomenon called mimetic desire — needed simple illustrations to reinforce its ideas. I'm such a visual thinker that I knew I would benefit from someone who could help me "see" the ideas in new ways. And I was betting that my readers would benefit from it, too.

The drawings needed to be no-frills, witty, biting, and thought-provoking to balance out the sometimes heavily philosophical nature of the text. I wanted art that would complement the writing, not just supplement it — art that didn't seem like a business class diagram or an unnecessary appendage.

Claire — along with hundreds of thousands of others — followed Liana on Instagram. Her work regularly appeared in the New Yorker, and she had just drawn the cover of Justin Bieber and Ariana Grande's new single, "Alone Together." More importantly, Claire said, it was like her drawings seemed to be winking about the very thing my book was about: mimetic, or imitative desire — the idea that our desires and identities are shaped by what other people want, the hidden dance of desire that influences our choices.

When I first laid eyes on Liana's drawings, I felt deeply understood — which is odd, considering that I'd never even met Liana or seen her work before. But there was something foundational to human experience that her art and my writing was trying to capture.

The Mechanics of Collaboration

Collaboration is one of the most mimetically complex human activities. Two people bring their own models, their own influences, their own ways of seeing — and they have to find a way to make something together that neither of them could have made alone.

"True collaboration is not the compromise of two visions. It is the discovery of a third thing that neither collaborator could have imagined independently."

The risk is that collaboration becomes a kind of mimetic convergence — that one person begins to imitate the other, and the result is merely an amplified version of the dominant voice. I had experienced this in other creative projects: the more forceful personality wins, and the "collaboration" is really just supervised execution.

With Liana, something different happened. Because she was working in a completely different medium — visual rather than verbal — there was no risk of her simply illustrating my ideas in the way I would have described them. She had to find her own way into the material, and in doing so, she found angles I hadn't seen.

What Wonder Does

The philosopher Josef Pieper wrote that wonder is the beginning of philosophy — that it is the experience of being confronted with something you cannot immediately assimilate into what you already know. Wonder stops you. It asks you to pause and pay attention.

Working with Liana gave me that experience repeatedly. She would send a drawing that captured something I had spent 2,000 words trying to articulate — and capture it in a single image, from an angle I hadn't considered. It was, in the best possible sense, disorienting.

Genuine collaboration produces a kind of productive disorientation — each collaborator is forced to see the shared subject through eyes other than their own.

This is, I now think, the deepest value of collaboration. Not efficiency. Not the pooling of complementary skills. But the encounter with an other — a real other, with a genuinely different way of seeing — that expands your own vision of what is possible.

The Anti-Mimetic Collaboration

There is a mimetic version of collaboration and an anti-mimetic one. The mimetic version seeks partners who confirm your existing vision, who amplify your existing voice, who make you more of what you already are. It is comfortable and productive in a narrow sense. But it doesn't produce wonder.

The anti-mimetic version seeks genuine difference — not conflict, but real otherness. It looks for a collaborator whose way of seeing is irreducibly their own, and trusts that the friction of two irreducible perspectives will generate something new.

That is harder to find and harder to sustain. It requires a kind of creative humility — the willingness to be surprised, to be corrected, to have your own vision expanded by someone who sees things you cannot. But the results, when it works, are extraordinary.

Wanting is a better book because of Liana Finck's illustrations. Not because they are beautiful (though they are), but because they showed me what my own ideas looked like from the outside — and in doing so, helped me understand them more deeply myself.

That is the wonder of collaboration: it gives you back your own work, transformed.
